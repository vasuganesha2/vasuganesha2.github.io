Statement
\[ f(x)=a_nx^n+a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\cdots+a_1x+a_0,\quad a_i\in\mathbb{Z} \] \[ p\nmid a_n,\qquad p\mid a_i\ (0\le i

One prime \(p\) must divide every coefficient except the leading one, and \(p^2\) must not divide the constant term. The same \(p\) works for all three conditions.

Checklist
✓ Leading \(a_n\): not divisible by \(p\).
✓ Middle \(a_{n-1},\dots,a_1\): divisible by \(p\).
✓ Constant \(a_0\): divisible by \(p\), not by \(p^2\).
Mirror version
\[ p\nmid a_0,\qquad p\mid a_i\ (1\le i\le n),\qquad p^2\nmid a_n \]

Eisenstein applied to the reversed polynomial \(x^nf(1/x)\). Useful when the prime sits on the leading coefficient instead.

Why it works
  1. Reduce to \(\mathbb{Z}[x]\). By Gauss's lemma, if \(f\) factors over \(\mathbb{Q}\), it factors over \(\mathbb{Z}\) as \(f=gh\) with \(\deg g=r\ge1\), \(\deg h=s\ge1\). (Dividing out the content keeps the hypotheses, since \(p\nmid a_n\).)
  2. Reduce mod \(p\). Only \(a_n\) survives: \[ \bar g\,\bar h=\bar a_n x^n \quad\text{in }\mathbb{F}_p[x]. \]
  3. Unique factorization. So \(\bar g=ux^r\) and \(\bar h=vx^s\); degrees don't drop because \(p\nmid a_n\).
  4. Contradiction. With \(r,s\ge1\), both constant terms vanish mod \(p\), so \[ p^2\mid g(0)\,h(0)=a_0, \] contradicting \(p^2\nmid a_0\). \(\blacksquare\)
A · Direct
\[ x^3+2x^2+4x+2 \]

Take \(p=2\): \(2\nmid1\), \(2\mid 2,4,2\), and \(4\nmid 2\). Irreducible.

Eisenstein's Criterion

A quick test for proving that a polynomial with integer coefficients is irreducible over the rationals.

polynomials irreducibility primes
01 The Criterion ▾
Statement
\[ f(x)=a_nx^n+a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\cdots+a_1x+a_0 \] If there exists a prime \(p\) such that \[ p\nmid a_n,\qquad p\mid a_i\ (0\le i
In words: the prime \(p\) divides every coefficient except the leading coefficient, and its square does not divide the constant term. The polynomial must have integer coefficients.
Coefficient checklist
✓ Leading coefficient: not divisible by \(p\).
✓ All other coefficients: divisible by \(p\).
✓ Constant coefficient: not divisible by \(p^2\).
If all three conditions hold for even one prime \(p\), irreducibility over \(\mathbb{Q}\) follows.
02 Worked Example ▾
Prove irreducibility
\[ f(x)=x^3+2x^2+4x+2 \]
Choose \(p=2\). The leading coefficient is \(1\), which is not divisible by \(2\). Every other coefficient (\(2,4,2\)) is divisible by \(2\), while the constant term \(2\) is not divisible by \(2^2=4\). Therefore, Eisenstein's criterion applies, so \(f(x)\) is irreducible over \(\mathbb{Q}\).
Check the conditions
\[ 2\nmid 1,\qquad 2\mid 2,4,2,\qquad 4\nmid 2. \]
03 Useful Trick: Shift the Variable ▾
When the criterion does not apply directly
A polynomial may fail the coefficient test in its original form but satisfy it after substituting \(x+1\) for \(x\), or more generally \(x+c\). If \(f(x+c)\) is irreducible over \(\mathbb{Q}\), then \(f(x)\) is also irreducible over \(\mathbb{Q}\), because translating the variable preserves factorization.
\[ f(x)\text{ irreducible} \iff f(x+c)\text{ irreducible} \qquad(c\in\mathbb{Q}). \]
Practical approach: expand \(f(x+1)\), inspect its coefficients, and look for a prime satisfying Eisenstein's conditions. This is a useful strategy, not a guarantee that such a shift exists.

B · Roots of a prime
\[ x^n-p \]

Middle coefficients are \(0\), constant is \(-p\), leading is \(1\). Irreducible for every \(n\), so \(\sqrt[n]{p}\) has degree exactly \(n\) over \(\mathbb{Q}\).

C · Mixed coefficients
\[ 3x^4+10x^3-15x^2+5x+20 \]

\(p=5\) divides \(10,-15,5,20\) but not \(3\), and \(25\nmid 20\). Tip: the gcd of the non-leading coefficients gives the candidate primes.

Translation invariance
\[ f(x)\text{ irreducible}\iff f(x+c)\text{ irreducible}\qquad(c\in\mathbb{Q}) \]

If Eisenstein fails for \(f\), expand \(f(x+1)\), \(f(x-1)\), \(f(x+2)\), … and look for a prime. A useful strategy, not a guarantee.

Common mistakes
  • One direction only. If no prime works, you learn nothing. \(x^2+1\) is irreducible but fails as written.
  • Integer coefficients. Clear denominators first: \(\tfrac13x^2+\tfrac12x+1\) becomes \(2x^2+3x+6\).
  • Same prime everywhere. You can't use \(p=2\) for one condition and \(p=3\) for another.
  • Check \(p^2\nmid a_0\). For \(x^2+4\), \(4\mid 4\), so \(p=2\) fails.
  • \(\mathbb{Q}\) vs \(\mathbb{Z}\). \(2x+2=2(x+1)\) factors in \(\mathbb{Z}[x]\) but is irreducible in \(\mathbb{Q}[x]\); irreducibility in \(\mathbb{Z}[x]\) needs primitivity.
